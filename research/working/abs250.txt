Government AI institutes and independent evaluators evaluate frontier systems to reduce reliance on developer self-reporting. Yet reporting a risk does not mitigate it. We examine whether published external evaluation findings about named frontier systems are acted upon.

We assembled 1,002 publicly documented findings from 438 reports by 47 evaluator organisations, published January 2023 to July 2026, spanning government institutes such as the UK AISI and US CAISI alongside independent evaluators. We trace downstream responses through three channels: company responses and model updates; parliamentary, congressional and regulatory action; and media and academic coverage. Of the 1,002 findings, 185 met our criteria for company-response analysis: a concerning empirical result about a named system for which a response was reasonable to expect. Under a predefined severity rubric, a three-model ensemble scoring each finding against eight dangerous-capability domains drawn from developers' own published thresholds, 147 crossed a threshold. Of these, 91 drew no publicly documented company response and 26 only a partial or acknowledgement-only one: 117 (80%) lacked a response proportionate to the risk identified. The shortfall does not depend on who reported the finding (75.9% government, 81.7% independent) or on obscurity (34 of the 91 unanswered findings drew press coverage), but it tracks access: pre-deployment findings went unanswered 12% of the time, against 81% post-deployment.

We then compare frontier-AI accountability structures with oversight regimes in food, drugs, energy, transportation and finance, and conclude that formal mechanisms for access, response, remediation, verification and follow-up could increase the consistency with which evaluations translate into action.
