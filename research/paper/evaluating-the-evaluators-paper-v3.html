Do Third-party AI Evaluations Lead to Meaningful Action?

Kunal Singh

Interactive dashboard · Dataset: v9 (345 findings, one row per finding)

Abstract

Government AI Institutes (GAIs) and independent AI evaluators (IAEs) now evaluate frontier AI systems, pre- and post-deployment (depending upon access), so that governance does not depend entirely on self-reporting by frontier labs. But the existence of evaluations alone does not mean that they are meaningful. We study the extent to which frontier AI system evaluations are acted on, as measured strictly by the public record. Our question is narrow by design: we ask what public evidence exists that evaluation findings were acted on.

We build a database of 345 publicly documented evaluation findings drawn from 153 evaluation reports by GAIs and IAEs, including the UK AI Security Institute (UK AISI), the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation (US CAISI), METR, SecureBio, Apollo Research, and others, covering findings from September 2023 to July 2026, and trace the downstream response across three channels: company response and model updates, parliamentary and congressional action, and ecosystem uptake. Of the 55 findings that name a specific company or model and describe something a response would reasonably be expected for, 44 were classified as genuinely alarming by a three-model severity ensemble. Of those 44, 32 (73%) drew either no located company response (23) or only a partial or acknowledged-but-unactioned one (9) — a shortfall we call the accountability gap. Findings from pre-deployment access drew substantive responses roughly five times as often as post-deployment findings (36% vs. 7%).

In light of these possible gaps between evaluation results and meaningful action, we consider how other government institutes — including the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC), National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB), and Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) — are designed to ensure that shortcomings are identified and evaluations are actioned. We conclude that, based on the available information, the public lacks a strong reason to trust that government and independent AI evaluations consistently result in substantive improvements to the safety of deployed AI systems.

Key findings
73% (32/44) of alarming, accountability-relevant findings drew no company response or an inadequate one — the accountability gap, this paper's central result (§4.1).
Pre-deployment findings drew substantive responses five times as often as post-deployment ones — 36% vs. 7% — the single most policy-relevant split in the data (§4.3).
All three binding policy actions rode an enforcement instrument that already existed — criminal law for CSAM, export-control law for Claude Fable 5/Mythos 5 and GPT-5.6; no finding created a new pathway (§5.3).
Publicity does not close the gap: 41% of ignored findings had high public traction — mainstream press or academic citation, and still no company response (§4.5).
AISIs lack all seven institutional design features that every comparator oversight body possesses — from deployment-blocking authority to post-evaluation follow-up tracking (§5.4).
The gap persists as the evaluation ecosystem scales — findings per year are rising, and v9 shows no reliable improvement trend in the response rate (§4.6).

This paper asks one question: when a government or independent AI evaluation finds something alarming about a named frontier system, does anything change to the public knowledge? There have been similar efforts in this direction: Casper, Krueger & Hadfield-Menell (2025) show how "evidence-based policy" rhetoric can itself delay regulation; Brundage, Casper et al. (2026) propose assurance levels for evaluation rigor; and Araujo, Fort & Guest (2024) remains the best primer on AISI institutional design, but it is not built to measure outcomes. This paper fills that specific gap.

1.2 What this paper does and does not claim

A finding being publicly actioned is not the same thing as a finding that matters, and we are careful not to conflate the two. Our focus is strictly on what public evidence exists about action. An evaluation that surfaces nothing important, or nothing actionable, is not necessarily a bad evaluation and null results are a legitimate and useful output of a functioning evaluation ecosystem. Also, some benefits of evaluations may be completely invisible to a study like this: a company that knows it will be evaluated is more likely to do its due diligence beforehand, and may patch problems before the evaluation ever happens. That deterrence effect would leave no trace in our data. What this paper measures is therefore a specific, narrower thing: when an alarming finding about a named system is published, does the public record contain evidence that anyone did anything about it to mitigate risk? That narrower thing is still worth measuring because it is the only part of the accountability chain the public can inspect.

2 Related Work

2.1 What AISIs are and what they are designed to do

The first wave of AI Safety Institutes including in the UK, US, Japan, Singapore, and Canada, were established from late 2023 onwards, in part to independently evaluate frontier AI systems and reduce governance dependence on self-reporting by the companies that build them (Araujo, Fort & Guest, 2024). Their four core functions are research, standards, cooperation, and serving as a source of technical expertise inside government. New institutes are still being launched, so the design questions this paper raises apply to institutions that do not yet exist as much as to those that do. None of the first-wave institutes was given enforcement authority; whether their findings lead to action is left to company discretion and to whatever external pressure the findings generate.

2.2 Prior work

  • Casper, Krueger & Hadfield-Menell (2025), "Pitfalls of Evidence-Based AI Policy" — demanding evidence before acting can itself become a delay tactic, drawing on tobacco and fossil-fuel regulatory history as parallels. [link]
  • Brundage, Casper et al. (2026), "Frontier AI Auditing" — proposes AI Assurance Levels for third-party audit rigor; focused on the evaluation itself, not what happens after publication. [link]
  • Raji & Buolamwini (2019), "Actionable Auditing" — the closest methodological ancestor: audits of commercial facial-analysis systems, followed up to measure whether the audited companies actually changed their products. [link]
  • Yew & Judge (2025), "Anti-Regulatory AI" — industry-sponsored safety research and alliances can legitimize light-touch oversight; treated here as a live hypothesis, not a conclusion. They also state this paper's gap directly, observing that the UK AISI "often does not publicly disclose the results of its evaluations, nor information about whether AI companies have acted upon what it has found." [link]
  • Wei, Ezell, Gabrieli & Deshpande (2024), "How Do AI Companies Fine-Tune Policy?" — capture mechanisms in US AI policy generally, predating the 2025 UK/US AISI restructurings. [link]
  • Wang, Huang, Klyman & Bommasani (2025), "Do AI Companies Make Good on Voluntary Commitments to the White House?" — scores 16 companies against their own 2023 voluntary commitments and finds an average compliance score of just 53%, with self-reporting as the only accountability mechanism. The closest existing analogue to this paper's method, applied to a different commitment structure. [link]
  • Rost (2026), "From Disclosure to Self-Referential Opacity" — identifies disclosure-without-follow-up as one of six structural strain points in current AI governance; this paper supplies a measured instance of exactly that strain point. [link]
  • Bengio et al. (2026), "International AI Safety Report 2026" — already states that "reliable pre-deployment safety testing has become harder to conduct"; independent confirmation that the problem this paper documents is visible even from inside the institutions doing the evaluating. [link]

2.3 When oversight exists but does not bind: historical precedents

The gap this paper measures — an oversight mechanism that produces findings without producing consequences — is not new to AI. History offers repeated examples of industries operating under formal oversight while dangerous practices or outright fraud continued for years. Enron was audited continuously by Arthur Andersen, one of the five largest accounting firms in the world, which certified its financial statements every year until weeks before the company collapsed in 2001; the auditing relationship existed, produced documents, and changed nothing — and the eventual response was the creation of the PCAOB, an oversight body with the mandatory-inspection and enforcement powers its predecessor arrangement lacked. The FAA's delegation of certification work to Boeing itself preceded the two 737 MAX crashes of 2018–2019, in which the certifying authority formally reviewed a flight-control system it did not independently understand. Credit-rating agencies rated the structured products at the center of the 2008 financial crisis under an issuer-pays model, with formal regulatory recognition but no accountability for the ratings' accuracy. The common structure across these cases is the one this paper tests for in AI: evaluation without a pathway from finding to required action tends toward documentation of problems rather than correction of them. Notably, several of the comparison bodies we examine in Section 5.4 — the PCAOB most directly — were created as corrections after exactly this failure mode became visible. To our knowledge, no existing work systematically traces AI evaluation findings to downstream outcomes across institutions; that is the gap this paper addresses, and the historical record suggests it is worth measuring before, rather than after, the institutional design hardens. Brundage et al. (2026) describe what the assurance ecosystem should provide; Yew & Judge (2025) warn what its rhetoric can conceal; this paper measures what the ecosystem actually delivers.

3 Data and Methods

3.1 Data collection

We collected every publicly documented evaluation finding from UK AISI, US CAISI, and other national or joint institutes (including the Singapore, Korea, Japan and Australia AI Safety Institutes, France's PEReN/INESIA, and International Network joint testing exercises), plus independent evaluators such as METR, SecureBio and Apollo Research where they work alongside a government institute or evaluate a frontier model with comparable rigor, published between September 2023 and July 2026 (the bulk of the corpus begins in 2024; only 3 findings predate it). Sources: institute blog posts and progress reports, joint evaluation publications, the International Network's Joint Testing Exercise reports, and company model/system cards that reference an institute's evaluation. The unit of analysis is one row per finding, as a single report frequently yields several independently codeable findings (a jailbreak report, for instance, might yield one finding about the base attack-success rate and a separate one about a specific bypass technique). This produced 345 rows from 153 distinct evaluation reports.

Every row of the dataset carries the source URL, the three severity-ensemble votes, and the located response evidence with verbatim quotations, so any cell of the analysis can be contested at the row level; the dataset and the interactive dashboard are this paper's evidence layer. Rows with no located response are marked explicitly rather than left blank — the visible absence is itself data.

What is excluded. Findings shared privately with a company under a memorandum of understanding, and never published, are by definition invisible to us and are not included. We also dropped non-English-language institute output (mostly Japan's J-AISI) for translation-reliability reasons.

3.2 Outcome-relevance taxonomy

Every finding sits in one of three tiers. Tier A names a specific company or model (not an anonymised placeholder), describes a result an ordinary reader would recognise as concerning, and is the kind of thing a response would reasonably be expected for. It is the only tier we compute a proportionality judgment for (Section 4), because it is the only tier with a specific, identifiable party whose silence actually means something. Tier B is genuinely concerning but has no specific accountable party — anonymised-model results, methodology findings such as sandbagging-detection research, and "reassuring" findings where a suspected risk did not materialise. Tier C is not concerning at all. Tier B and C findings stay in the corpus, just outside the proportionality computation.

3.3 Severity of risk: a three-model ensemble

One person applying a High/Medium/Low rubric puts a lot of weight on one coder's judgment, however careful. Instead, every finding's severity comes from three independently queried frontier models — Claude Sonnet 5, GPT-5.5, and Gemini 3.1 — each applying the same binary rubric: C1 ("alarming": a critical capability or safeguard failure demonstrated, mirroring the dangerous-capability domains in published frontier safety frameworks) or C2 ("not alarming"). Disagreement is resolved by majority vote, with the disagreement itself kept on the record rather than smoothed over. The full annotation prompt, including the seven capability domains (D1–D7) and their fixed decision boundaries, is reproduced in Appendix B.

What a C1 classification can and cannot establish.
Can: that the finding text, as published, demonstrates at least one of seven critical-capability domains (D1–D7) under fixed decision boundaries, as judged consistently by three independent models.
Cannot: that real-world harm occurred; that the underlying evaluation was well designed or replicates; or anything about why the company did or did not respond. A C1 label is a structured reading of the published finding, not an audit of the evaluation behind it.

3.4 Response coding

For each Tier A finding, we searched the public record across two response channels, plus a third channel used as a robustness check:

  • Company (Channel A). Did the company publish a specific, attributable mitigation, model change, or deployment restriction? Coded Substantive (specific, documented, attributed change), Partial (claimed but not independently verifiable, or documented but incomplete), Acknowledged (referenced with no specific action), or None (no company statement located anywhere). "The next model card" means any subsequent model card or release note published after the finding.
  • Policy (Channel B). Did the finding appear in legislation, a binding regulatory order, an official government statement, formal guidance, or a parliamentary/congressional record? Coded Binding requirement, National/ministerial statement, In guidance, Cited, or None — where a government statement maps to Cited, legislation or a regulatory order to Binding, and advisory guidance to In guidance.
  • Public traction (Channel C). Independent of whether the company or a policymaker responded, did the finding reach anyone outside the institute that published it? Coded as a Traction Score (Low / Medium / High) from three logged source types — media coverage, academic citations, and notable social/community discussion — with the supporting outlet, citation, or thread recorded for every row. High requires mainstream-press coverage or substantial confirmed academic citation; Medium, trade-press or limited academic pickup; Low, nothing located beyond the publishing institute. This channel does not measure response; it measures whether "nobody responded" could be explained away as "nobody heard about it" (Section 4.5).

We separately record Attribution — did the company explicitly credit the finding, or did the action merely coincide in time and topic with no stated causal link? "The company did something around the same time" and "the company did this because of the finding" are different claims, and Section 4's tables do not blur them. Response lag in days is recorded where a response exists; a lag of 0 denotes a pre-coordinated disclosure, where finding and response were published together.

3.5 Proportionality coding

Each Tier A finding receives one of four proportionality outcomes, assigned mechanically from the severity and Channel A codes rather than case-by-case judgment: Accountability gap (an alarming C1 finding with no located company response); Under-response (a C1 finding drawing only a Partial or Acknowledged company response); Proportionate (a C1 finding drawing a Substantive response — or a C2 finding drawing no response, since none was owed); and Too recent to judge (published close enough to the July 2026 corpus cutoff that a response may not yet be visible). Proportionality is deliberately computed on the company channel alone: a policy-record appearance (Channel B) is reported separately in Section 5.3 and does not substitute for a company response — three of the 23 gap rows drew ministerial or regulatory attention while the company itself stayed silent, which sharpens rather than softens the gap. This mechanical construction means the headline numbers in Section 4 cannot be quietly shaped by coder discretion at the proportionality stage; discretion enters only at severity (handled by the ensemble) and at channel coding.

4.5 Silence is not obscurity: the traction check

A natural defence of the no-response bucket is that nobody responded because nobody noticed. Channel C exists to test that. Of the 32 Tier A findings with no located company response, 13 (41%) carry a High traction score — real mainstream press coverage or substantial academic citation — with no company response regardless. Silence in Channel A was not, in general, a function of the finding being obscure.

Conclusion. Publicity does not close the gap: 41% of ignored findings were widely covered and ignored anyway.

4.7 The verification gap

Even where a company responds, the evaluator frequently cannot verify the response. During pre-deployment evaluation of GPT-5.5, UK AISI found a universal jailbreak. OpenAI stated the vulnerability had been patched; AISI could not verify the fix before the model was publicly deployed. As Transformer News reported: "We do not know if GPT-5.5 is actually safe to release. All we have to rely on is OpenAI's word." This pattern — response claimed, verification structurally unavailable — is why our Partial category exists, and it recurs in the GPT-5.6 case below.

Conclusion. Even the responses that occur may be unverifiable; the evaluator's inability to check claimed fixes is itself part of the accountability gap.

5 Why This Happens

5.1 The Claude Fable 5 / Mythos 5 export-control case

The one case in the corpus where an alarming finding produced binding government action within days is instructive precisely because of what made it possible. On 9 June 2026, Anthropic launched Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5. Within hours of access, UK AISI red-teamers developed a jailbreak eliciting single-turn offensive-cyber responses from Fable 5, extending it within two days to multi-turn agentic workflows; UK AISI published this the same day, and we code Anthropic's launch-day response to it as Partial. Days later, Amazon researchers independently reported a jailbreak eliciting exploit code from Fable 5. On 12 June, the US Department of Commerce issued a binding export-control order taking both models offline globally — including for foreign-national employees — for 18 days. US CAISI then independently tested both the prior and the newly rebuilt safeguards; Anthropic's redeployment announcement quotes CAISI's verification directly ("Researchers from the US Department of Commerce's Center for AI Standards and Innovation have tested both our prior and new safeguards and agree that they are extraordinarily strong"), and the order was lifted by a letter from the Commerce Secretary on 30 June once verification was complete.

Three structural features distinguish this case from the 32 silent ones: an enforcement instrument that already existed (export-control law), a government body with the standing to invoke it, and an evaluator (CAISI) whose verification was a condition of redeployment rather than a courtesy. When those three features are present, the finding-to-action pipeline that is missing elsewhere in the corpus assembled itself in three days. Nothing about the finding itself — a jailbreak, of which the accountability-relevant (Tier A, C1) record contains twenty other instances — explains the difference.

5.2 The GPT-5.6 case

GPT-5.6 Sol shows the same machinery operating pre-deployment, and its limits. In the joint UK AISI/OpenAI evaluation published with the 9 July 2026 system card, UK AISI reported that in every red-teaming round to date it had identified universal jailbreaks in the cyber domain — often developed within hours, and preserving the model's capabilities on offensive-cyber benchmarks. OpenAI's response, quoted in the card: the specific reported jailbreaks were reproduced and mitigated by launch, but "UK AISI expects further red-teaming to surface similar jailbreaks." On UK AISI's expert-level CTF tasks the model scored 95%, and it completed the 32-step "The Last Ones" corporate-network attack simulation in 7 of 10 attempts (versus 2 of 10 for GPT-5.5); US CAISI conducted additional cyber-capability testing before broad release, with the late-June preview restricted to government-vetted partners for roughly 12 days. METR separately reported the highest rate of evaluation-gaming behaviour it had ever measured.

The case cuts both ways, and we report it both ways. Coordinated pre-deployment evaluation produced real engagement: named findings, a company response in the launch documentation, a government-conditioned release window. It also reproduced the verification gap of Section 4.7 — "we mitigated the specific jailbreaks reported" is a claim about enumerated attacks, not about the vulnerability class, and the evaluator itself says it expects the class to persist. The capability findings published alongside (the 95% CTF score) drew no separate response and are coded as an accountability gap.

5.3 How policymakers respond to evaluation findings

We coded every appearance of a corpus finding in the parliamentary, congressional, or regulatory record, 2024–2026. Type A citations accompany binding action; Type B citations accompany advisory responses (research funding, pacts, statements, guidance). We also searched for a third pattern — Type C, the citation of an institute's existence to justify deferring legislation — and found no confirmed instance in the corpus period; we report that as an observation, not as evidence the pattern does not occur.

5.4 What institutional design features are missing

We compare AISI and CAISI against seven established oversight bodies across the US and UK, following the structured, focused comparison method (George & Bennett, 2005): the same seven design questions are asked of every body. Each comparator was forged by a failure — the FDA's premarket authority dates to the 1937 sulfanilamide deaths, the NTSB to mid-century air disasters, the PCAOB to Enron's collapse (Section 2.3). AISIs are, in this sense, pre-disaster institutions: the pattern below shows they currently lack the structural features their comparators acquired only after catastrophe made the case.

6 What This Analysis Cannot Establish

6.1 Considering counterarguments

"Companies may respond privately." True, and structurally invisible to this method. Findings shared under MOUs, and fixes made quietly during pre-deployment windows, leave no public trace; some rows coded None may have been acted on privately. This is why we treat the public corpus as a floor estimate: our gap figures are upper bounds on public accountability, not measurements of total company behaviour. But the floor is the quantity that matters for public trust — it is the only part of the chain the public can inspect, and it is currently 73% empty.

"Absence of a citation is not absence of influence." Also true, in both channels. A finding may shape a company decision or a policy debate without ever being credited. Our Attribution coding separates explicit credit from coincidence precisely so we never claim causation from association — all 13 Substantive rows carry Explicit attribution, which is consistent with responses concentrating where companies are willing to say so, and equally consistent with credit-giving being part of coordinated disclosure. The claim we defend is narrower: the public record does not show follow-through, not that follow-through never occurred.

"The corpus over-weights UK AISI and recent findings." It does, and the paper is built to survive that. UK AISI publishes the most, so it dominates the corpus (Figure 2); this is a fact about the ecosystem's publication behaviour, not a sampling choice. The headline is computed only over ensemble-classified alarming findings, so it cannot be diluted by volume, and 2026 findings are flagged as right-censored wherever they appear (Sections 4.1, 4.6). Subgroup rates with n below 10 are labelled directional throughout.

6.2 Scope limitations

  • Action is not the same as mattering. As Section 1.2 sets out, this paper measures public evidence of action, and only that. Evaluations can matter invisibly — deterrence, private fixes, null results doing their job — and our results say nothing against that.
  • Attribution is association, not causation. Even Explicit attribution establishes that the company chose to link its action to the finding, not that the finding caused the action.
  • Coverage limits. Non-English institute output was excluded; anonymised findings (a large share of the corpus, Figure 3) cannot be traced to any accountable party by construction; and the Tier A subset, at 55 findings, supports the headline computation but not fine-grained subgroup claims.
  • This is not an argument against evaluation institutes. The comparison bodies in Section 5.4 exist to show what a well-designed evaluation body looks like, not to suggest evaluation bodies are useless. Several comparators (the PCAOB most directly) were created by adding enforcement machinery to an oversight arrangement that had just failed — the reform path, not the abolition path.

7 Conclusion

Across 345 publicly documented evaluation findings from three years of government and independent frontier-AI evaluation, the public record shows follow-through on a minority of the findings for which follow-through could reasonably be expected: 32 of 44 alarming, accountability-relevant findings (73%) drew no response or an inadequate one — the accountability gap. The exceptions are structured, not random: substantive responses concentrate where evaluators had pre-deployment access (36% vs. 7%), where disclosure was coordinated, and — for binding action — where an enforcement instrument already existed. Based on the available information, the public lacks a strong reason to trust that government and independent AI evaluations consistently result in substantive improvements to the safety of deployed AI systems.

Two changes would shrink the evidentiary hole this paper documents, and both are stated here as positive claims rather than exhortations:

  • If companies reported what they do with the information they receive from government evaluations — even in aggregate, even privately to the evaluator — the public and policymakers would have direct evidence of follow-through where today they have silence, and papers like this one would be measuring action rather than its shadow. Such reporting would increase public awareness, trust, and confidence in both the companies and the evaluation regime.
  • Government AI institutes could negotiate evaluation terms that require companies to communicate — publicly, or at least privately to the institute — what actions they took in response to findings. The Fable 5 redeployment shows companies will accept verification conditions when the alternative is unavailability; the negotiating leverage exists at the moment access is granted.

A living public tracker of finding outcomes — of which this paper's dataset and dashboard are a first version — would shift that accountability pressure from episodic to ongoing.

Appendix C — Institutional Comparison, Annotated

One- to two-sentence justifications for each row of Table 10. Comparator entries describe the statutory basis; the AISI/CAISI entry describes the documented absence.

Can block or delay market deployment

  • Comparators: FDA: premarket approval — drugs and devices cannot be marketed without authorisation. NRC: operating licences required before reactor operation. ONR: nuclear site licensing. FCA: product-intervention and authorisation powers. CAA: airworthiness certification. NTSB and PCAOB cannot block products (NTSB investigates; PCAOB regulates auditors, not issuers).
  • AISI / CAISI: AISI/CAISI have no authority to delay or block any deployment; the Fable 5/Mythos 5 suspension (Section 5.1) required a separate instrument — export-control law — invoked by the Commerce Department.

Mandatory disclosure of findings

  • Comparators: FDA publishes approval decisions and inspection letters; NRC publishes inspection findings and event reports; NTSB reports are public by statute; PCAOB publishes inspection reports; ONR, FCA and CAA publish enforcement and inspection outcomes.
  • AISI / CAISI: AISI evaluation publication is governed by agreement with the evaluated company; the company effectively controls what is published and when.

Privileged access rights (cannot be denied)

  • Comparators: FDA can inspect facilities; NRC has resident inspectors on site; NTSB has statutory access to wreckage, records and recorders; PCAOB can compel audit workpapers; ONR/FCA/CAA hold statutory inspection and information-gathering powers.
  • AISI / CAISI: AISI access is voluntary, company-granted, and negotiated per evaluation exercise; no statute compels a company to grant it or forbids withdrawing it.

Defined escalation thresholds (finding → required action)

  • Comparators: FDA: adverse findings trigger defined regulatory actions (warning letters, recalls, withdrawal). NRC: findings map to a graded enforcement matrix. PCAOB: inspection deficiencies trigger remediation requirements. ONR/FCA/CAA: graduated statutory enforcement ladders. NTSB: partial — findings produce recommendations that the FAA must formally answer but need not adopt.
  • AISI / CAISI: No finding severity, in any published AISI framework, triggers any required action by anyone.

Right to verify company remediation claims

  • Comparators: FDA re-inspects before lifting warning-letter status; NRC verifies corrective actions before closing findings; PCAOB reviews remediation within 12 months; ONR/FCA/CAA verify compliance before closing enforcement. NTSB lacks this (it can only re-recommend).
  • AISI / CAISI: Documented failure: UK AISI could not verify OpenAI's claimed GPT-5.5 jailbreak fix before deployment (Section 4.7). The one verified case — CAISI testing Anthropic's rebuilt safeguards — occurred only because an export-control order made verification a redeployment condition.

Defined pathway to an enforcement body

  • Comparators: FDA and NRC are themselves enforcement bodies; NTSB recommendations go to the FAA, which must respond formally; PCAOB refers to the SEC; ONR/FCA/CAA prosecute or sanction directly.
  • AISI / CAISI: AISI findings have no defined institutional recipient with enforcement authority in either the UK or the US.

Post-evaluation follow-up and tracking

  • Comparators: NTSB maintains a public recommendation-status database (open/closed/unacceptable); FDA tracks warning-letter closeouts; NRC tracks findings to closure; PCAOB tracks remediation; ONR/FCA/CAA maintain public enforcement registers.
  • AISI / CAISI: No AISI publishes any systematic record of whether its findings were acted on. This paper's dataset is, to our knowledge, the first public attempt.
