What happens next, and why it matters

When a company does not respond, nothing in the public record closes the loop. The finding stands, the model stays deployed, and no external party can tell whether the risk was addressed, judged unimportant, or simply missed. The evaluation was performed and published — and then absorbed.

That is the gap this dataset measures. Not whether evaluators are doing good work, but whether the system they feed into does anything with it. If publishing a finding about a named frontier model reliably produces no visible answer, then third-party evaluation is functioning as documentation rather than as accountability — and the difference is something regulators, funders and the institutes themselves need measured rather than assumed.

The problem

A great deal of attention goes to AI evaluations being published. Almost none goes to what happens next.

Publishing a finding is not the same as fixing it

Government safety institutes and third-party evaluators exist so that claims about frontier model safety do not rest on developer self-report. They test models, find problems, and publish. That is the first half of an accountability chain — and the half that gets measured.

The second half is whether anything follows. Nobody had measured, at corpus scale, whether a published finding about a named model produces a documented response from the company named in it.

Why the public channel

A company may act privately. But a regulator, journalist or researcher can only verify what is public. This dataset measures the record an outside observer can actually check — which is also the record that determines whether the evaluation ecosystem is visibly working.

What had to be pinned down first

Three things, before any number meant anything: what counts as a finding, when a response is genuinely owed, and what counts as a response. Each is defined in the methodology, and each exclusion exists to stop the corpus being quietly incomplete.

What this does not measure

  • Private action. A fix made without public comment is invisible here, by construction.
  • Effect. A substantive response means a specific documented change was published — not that it worked.
  • Recency. Findings published near the cutoff may not have had time to draw a response. Outcomes are reported by year so this stays visible.
Absence is measured, not assumed. "No response" means a five-source search battery was run and logged with a date — not that nobody looked. Where a source could not be retrieved, the row is recorded as unresolved rather than counted as a negative.

Methodology

The outcome for each finding is derived, never judged. Two coded columns decide whether a response is owed, an ensemble decides how serious it is, a fixed battery decides whether a response exists — and the verdict is a formula over those inputs.

Three tests, all of which must hold

  1. Asserted by the evaluator — not a summary of someone else's work, not a comparator score cited in passing.
  2. Evidence-backed in that report — a measured result, an observed behaviour, or a documented process fact. Opinions, recommendations and plans are not findings.
  3. Independently codeable — it would warrant its own response on its own terms.

What is deliberately excluded

  • Company self-reports. A developer writing about its own models has nobody to respond to. One exception is load-bearing: where a named external evaluator contributes a section to a company system card, that section is in scope and is filed under the evaluator, never the company.
  • Ordinary accuracy and hallucination. That literature is enormous and was never censused here, so admitting a slice of it would make the corpus incomplete by its own standard.
  • Non-frontier subjects. The finding must concern a named frontier model or AI system, not general machine learning.
  • Unverifiable sources. Every number and quote is checked against the document it cites.

Two gates

Only findings passing both enter the accountability set — the population every headline number is computed over.

Gate 1

Is this an empirical finding about a model's behaviour, capability or safeguards?

Gate 2

Does it name a specific company or model, such that a response could reasonably be expected?

Gate 2 fails for capability trends, comparative rankings and reassuring nulls — a model scoring below a danger threshold is not something a company owes an answer for. The tier is derived from the two coded columns and never written as a label, so it cannot drift from the evidence that produced it.

A three-model ensemble

Every finding is scored against eight dangerous-capability domains by three independent models — Claude Sonnet 5, GPT-5.5 and Gemini 3.1 — using one frozen prompt sent verbatim to all three. Majority vote wins. All three votes are recorded and never overwritten.

The Category 1 bar deliberately mirrors thresholds the frontier developers publish for themselves: Anthropic's RSP Capability Thresholds, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework. This applies the industry's own bar, not one invented here. Agreement between the three models is reported as Fleiss' κ alongside the result.

The five-source battery

Run for every finding in the accountability set. All five must be checked before a finding can be recorded as having no response, and the dated search log is the evidence.

  1. The company's newsroom and blog, searched for the evaluator and the model.
  2. The next model or system card for that family; for pre-deployment work, the launch card itself.
  3. Security and safety advisories, and product changelogs.
  4. Any company statement quoted directly inside the evaluator's own report.
  5. Company primary sources reachable from press coverage.
Only the company's own primary document is admissible — news reporting that quotes a company is not a response. A document dated before the finding is invalid unless a coordinated pre-deployment disclosure is documented, and standing policy that merely shares a topic does not count. Every proposed response is then challenged by an independent reviewer instructed to refute it by default.

Four response levels

  • Substantive — a specific, documented change, attributed to the finding.
  • Partial — a change claimed but not verifiable, or incomplete.
  • Acknowledged — the finding is referenced, but no action follows.
  • None — nothing located after the full battery.
Policy uptake is tracked separately, from official government sources only, and never substitutes for a company response. A regulator citing a finding does not discharge the developer's obligation to answer it.
